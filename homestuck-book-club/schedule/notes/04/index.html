Reading overview

Hivebent starts over with twelve trolls on Alternia. The former chat-window antagonists become the main cast, with their own interests, lusii, feuds, romances, and competing plans for Sgrub. Their supposed two-team competition ultimately forms one connected session.

The group wins its battle against the Black King and creates the humans’ universe, but an intruding demon prevents them from claiming their reward. They retreat to the Veil. Their anger at the humans now has a context, though blame and causality remain much messier than Karkat’s insults suggested.

> Read Recap #4.

Reading notes

  • TROLL INTRODUCTIONS YAY. I have been waiting for the drama to take over. Karkat’s passion for terrible romantic movies makes him feel much closer to John than his earlier trolling suggested. Watching his attempts at leadership adds another layer of awkwardness. The change of perspective humanizes him, for lack of a better word.
  • Relationships get more than one category. The quadrant explanation treats attraction, hostility, and forms of care that humans might call platonic as parts of troll romance. It even gives mediation its own relationship role. I like seeing so much importance given to relationships beyond a conventional romantic couple. Having a name for a relationship does not, by itself, make it caring or mutual.
  • Equius’s control is gross. His policing of Nepeta’s language and his attempt to program Aradia’s feelings are worth discussing as specific acts of control. Nepeta also challenges his claim to be above her games: she points out how much he plays with his own typing style. She notices his hypocrisy even when he insists on having the last word.
  • The hierarchy is not just decoration. Alternia’s social ranks shape how the trolls treat one another. Equius combines prejudice with deference to those above him, while Eridan and Feferi want very different futures for their society. I want to compare what each character defends, what they want to change, and what they seem to take for granted.
  • Who gets to call something inevitable? Doc Scratch downplays his manipulations as small nudges toward events that would happen anyway. Yet he is actively encouraging Vriska to hurt people. Aradia’s memos raise a different problem: even her own future selves become authorities telling her what to do. How much room does either version of inevitability leave for a choice?
  • Moirail breakup :( Eridan offers to drop his typing accent to show he is serious, and Feferi answers in plain letters too. I love how stepping out of the typing bit becomes an emotional gesture. If we made a club shipping wall, I would want it to show one-sided feelings, contested labels, and changing relationships, not just declare every pairing mutual.

Discussion questions

  1. Which troll did you change your mind about most once we saw their life on Alternia?
  2. Which relationship did you find yourself most invested in, and why?
  3. How much credit does Karkat deserve for holding the group together?
  4. What stays recognizably Aradia as her form changes, and what seems to change with it?
  5. When do quadrant labels clarify what the trolls want from each other, and when do they obscure it?
  6. What does Feferi owe Eridan as his moirail, and where should that responsibility end?
  7. How does Alternia’s hierarchy show up in ordinary conversations? Where do the trolls defend it, resist it, or take it for granted?
  8. The trolls create a universe but cannot enter it. What does their interrupted victory suggest about the game’s promise of a reward?

Trivia & Details

  • Hivebent begins with familiar ages. The opening translates six solar sweeps into thirteen Earth years. The trolls are the same age as the kids, even before their introductions start echoing the kids’ rooms and interests.
  • The names have a visible pattern. Each of the twelve introduced trolls has a six-letter first name and a six-letter surname. Their trolltag initials also stay within A, C, G, and T, the letters used for DNA’s four bases. Try checking the pattern against the introductions.
  • Karkat’s outdoor monologue rewrites John’s. Compare John’s lyrical monologue with Karkat dismissing poetry and mailbox arms. The echo is in the wording, not just the visual setup.
  • Kanaya does not know where the walkthrough came from. She imagines it is a record of an ancient alien civilization. We need to distinguish her explanation of the journal from what the story has actually established about Rose’s place in the timeline.
