Reading overview

Jade’s introduction opens up another way of experiencing the story: reminders, gadgets, dreams, and knowledge of events that have not happened yet. John experiments with alchemy and learns that he has misread Dad. Rose and Dave complete the next steps in their entry chain while the exiles’ packages and royal errands keep intersecting with the kids’ lives.

Then the Intermission hands the story to Spades Slick and the Midnight Crew. Their raid on the Felt’s mansion turns a gang feud into a time-travel puzzle. Injuries, effigies, and broken clocks become clues to when events happen and how the characters affect one another.

Reading notes

  • Jade’s introduction is so good. The narration feels more playful and distracted around her. Her colored-string reminders are funny because she can anticipate future events but still needs help remembering what to do. I also appreciate how lovingly her fantasy of becoming a wolf is described, even while the comic is making jokes about it.
  • Commands as an internal conversation. A command followed by a character’s objection can feel like someone having a thought and immediately arguing with it. At other times, the command feels like an unwanted suggestion from outside. I like the uncertainty about whether we are hearing a character’s thoughts or watching them answer someone else.
  • The dreambot is a bridge. One page pairs Jade in her dream room with her robot standing beside her sleeping body. I love how concrete that makes the connection between dreaming and waking life. Prospit feels less like an escape from everyday life than another place where actions have consequences.
  • One consciousness at a time? Switching characters feels more conspicuous here than a scene change in a novel. The commands ask us to “be” someone else, and the rooms often look like spaces in a game. Moving to another character can feel like changing which person we control, even though we cannot choose what they do.
  • The Intermission is a primer in time trouble. Time becomes something the characters can follow through a room: past and future trails give it a visible path. Slick even uses a clock he has seen broken in the future to anticipate what is about to happen. I love the inventiveness of this, but the body-shaming jokes are rough.

Discussion questions

  1. What did Jade’s introduction make you want to know about her?
  2. How did you feel about leaving the kids for the Intermission? Was there a moment that changed your reaction?
  3. What does Jade learn on Prospit, and how does that explain some of her behavior on Earth?
  4. Spades Slick looks like Jack Noir. What connection, if any, do you think the story is setting up?
  5. Whose bodies become punchlines in the Intermission, and how does that affect the humor for you?
  6. How does the Intermission teach us to follow its time travel? Which details help you work out the order of events?
  7. What would Homestuck lose if the Intermission were left out?

Trivia & Details

  • Becquerel is a unit as well as a dog. Jade’s pet is named Becquerel. A becquerel measures radioactive activity: one decay per second. It is a tidy connection with the comic’s recurring nuclear imagery.
  • The inventory joke survives the cast change. Asked to captchalogue a clock, Slick does not recognize the term. The interface is familiar to us, but the character does not share the kids’ vocabulary.
  • The tailor repairs the fabric of spacetime. Time travel causes tears in Lord English’s coat, which Stitch has to mend. The familiar phrase becomes an actual tailoring problem.
  • The simple option is rejected on purpose. John’s array modus permits direct access to any card. The narration immediately complains that it is boring. A practical interface would eliminate a lot of Homestuck’s early plot.
