Reading overview

Jade’s introduction opens up another way of experiencing the story: reminders, gadgets, dreams, and knowledge of events that have not happened yet. John experiments with alchemy and learns that he has misread Dad. Rose and Dave complete the next steps in their entry chain while the exiles’ packages and royal errands keep intersecting with the kids’ lives.

Then the Intermission hands the story to Spades Slick and the Midnight Crew. Their raid on the Felt’s mansion turns a gang feud into a tangle of time travel, alternate outcomes, damaged effigies, and very consequential clocks.

Reading notes

  • Jade’s introduction is so good. The narration feels more playful and distracted around her. Her colored-string reminders make an everyday memory aid oddly mysterious. I also appreciate how lovingly the animal-identification fantasy is described, even while the comic is making jokes about it.
  • Commands as an internal conversation. The prompt and response can feel like thought and counterthought, or an intrusive suggestion being rejected. Is the narrator interacting with a character, borrowing their consciousness, or both?
  • The dreambot is a bridge. One page pairs Jade in her dream room with her robot standing beside her sleeping body. I love how concrete that makes the connection between dreaming and waking life. Prospit feels less like an escape from everyday life than another place where actions have consequences.
  • One consciousness at a time? The rapid character changes feel more conspicuous than a novel’s scene changes to me. Is that because a page often gives us one tightly framed point of view? The game-like, often isometric rooms make the act of switching characters feel literal.
  • The Intermission is a primer in time trouble. Past and future trails turn chronology into something characters navigate spatially. Even the anti-clock vendetta becomes a way to act on a timeline. Also: the body-shaming jokes are rough. I want to discuss that alongside the inventive structure, rather than write it off as background noise.

Discussion questions

  1. Which of the four kids are you most interested in after Jade's introduction?
  2. Which aspect of Jade's life seems most significant to you?
  3. How did the Intermission affect your engagement with the story?
  4. Which new idea introduced in this section changed your understanding of Homestuck the most?
  5. How does Jade’s knowledge of future events shape her role among the four kids? Where are its limits?
  6. What does the concept of a dreamself add to the characters as we understand them so far?
  7. Why does Homestuck spend so much time following objects, packages, and other forms of transmission?
  8. How does Jack Noir differ from the other inhabitants of the game world?
  9. How does the Intermission change your sense of what counts as part of the main narrative?
  10. How does Homestuck use time travel to structure information for the reader?
  11. What effect does the characters' limited knowledge have on the way we interpret events?
  12. How does repetition change the meaning of events or objects when they reappear?
  13. What does the Intermission suggest about the role of digression in Homestuck?
  14. Spades Slick resembles Jack Noir. What does the Intermission actually establish about that resemblance, and what are we still guessing?
  15. Whose bodies become punchlines in the Intermission? Does that change how the humor lands for you?

Trivia & Details

  • The inventory joke survives the cast change. Asked to captchalogue a clock, Slick does not recognize the term. The interface is familiar to us, but the character does not share the kids’ vocabulary.
  • The tailor repairs the fabric of spacetime. Stitch’s work on Lord English’s coat makes that expression literal.
  • The simple option is rejected on purpose. John’s array modus permits direct access to any card. The narration immediately complains that it is boring. A practical interface would eliminate a lot of Homestuck’s early plot.
