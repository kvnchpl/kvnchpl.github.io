Reading overview

John tries to retrieve his birthday mail, play a game with his friends, and survive his own inventory system. Connecting to Rose through Sburb turns ordinary household objects into tools for escaping a meteor. John enters the Medium, where Nannasprite explains that the game is much bigger than either of them understood.

Rose and Dave face their own domestic obstacles while Jade seems to know more than she explains. Meanwhile, the Wayward Vagabond builds Can Town on a ruined future Earth. His terminal complicates the question of who has been giving John commands all along.

> Read Recap #1.

Reading notes

  • Who is “you”? I love how the second-person narration slides between instructions, inner monologue, and an audience the characters seem aware of. Rose refuses a prompt to fuss over her private journals because someone is watching. Later, one of the commands we have just read is shown being typed on an in-story keyboard. The command prompt is becoming part of the world itself.
  • They know things we do not. Captchaloguing is already ordinary life to these kids; we have to catch up. Then Sburb puts us and them in the same position of learning unfamiliar rules. Rose reading the walkthrough while John waits for help is a good miniature of that.
  • Rooms as character introductions. John, Rose, and Dave feel like distinct online archetypes before we know very much about them. Their possessions, typing, and arguments with guardians do a lot of the work. The strained parent-child relationships are worth comparing without assuming the kids have read their parents correctly.
  • The irony has levels, apparently. The description of Bro’s Mr. T puppet piles one level of irony onto another. Later, Dave finds some of Bro’s material too ironic to deal with. I am interested in where ironic distance becomes indistinguishable from sincere investment.
  • Alchemy as a storytelling principle. Sprites, punched cards, and household objects keep becoming combinations of things we already know. Even language is something to recombine: Dave renames his skateboard a “wheeled ride” to avoid an inventory collision. That feels very Andrew Hussie.

Discussion questions

  1. Which character has made the strongest impression on you so far?
  2. Which aspect of Sburb are you most curious about at this point?
  3. Which guardian-child relationship stands out most to you?
  4. What detail from the first two acts seems most likely to matter later?
  5. How are John, Rose, and Dave characterized through their rooms, possessions, and interests?
  6. How does the server-player relationship change the dynamics between the characters?
  7. What role do rules, instructions, and systems play in the characters' lives so far?
  8. How does Nannasprite's explanation of the game change the scale of the story?
  9. How does Homestuck use interfaces and game mechanics as part of its storytelling?
  10. How does the comic complicate the idea of who is in control of the story?
  11. How does presenting an apocalypse through the structure of a game affect the way the events are experienced?
  12. What reading habits does Homestuck seem to be teaching its audience?
  13. Jade says Rose and Dave do not always have to mean the opposite of what they say. When does irony help these friends connect, and when does it get in the way?

Trivia & Details

  • The fake literary credits are a running joke. The fireplace passage credits Mark Twain, but the moon stealing its light comes from Shakespeare’s Timon of Athens, Act 4, scene 3 (Folger text). Rose’s rainy introduction credits Charles Barkley for the opening of T. S. Eliot’s The Waste Land (Academy of American Poets). The narrator’s certainty is part of the joke.
  • [S] starts small. The piano page explicitly introduces [S] as the marker for sound. Compare that early cue with the act-ending animation, [S] WV: Ascend.
  • A word can change the mechanics. Dave gets his battery pack accepted by treating Y as a consonant instead of a vowel. The punchline depends on wording, rather than a new item or a stronger weapon.
