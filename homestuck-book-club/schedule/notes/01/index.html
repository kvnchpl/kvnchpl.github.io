Reading overview

John tries to retrieve his birthday mail, play a game with his friends, and survive his own inventory system. Connecting to Rose through Sburb turns ordinary household objects into tools for escaping a meteor. John enters the Medium, where Nannasprite explains that the game is much bigger than either of them understood.

Rose and Dave face their own domestic obstacles while Jade seems to know more than she explains. Meanwhile, the Wayward Vagabond builds Can Town on a ruined future Earth. His terminal complicates the question of who has been giving John commands all along.

> Read Recap #1.

Reading notes

  • Who is “you”? I love how “you” can address a character while the narration also seems to speak for that character’s thoughts. Sometimes the characters seem aware of us as readers, too. Rose rejects the suggestion to fuss over her private journals because someone is watching. Later, a command we have just read is shown being typed on an in-story keyboard. What first looked like a way to tell the story is becoming something that happens inside it.
  • They know things we do not. Captchaloguing is already ordinary life to these kids; we have to catch up. Sburb reverses that situation: now the kids also need someone to explain unfamiliar rules. Rose reading the walkthrough while John waits for help lets us learn about the game alongside them.
  • Rooms as character introductions. We learn a lot about John, Rose, and Dave through their possessions, typing styles, and arguments with their guardians. Their rooms establish personalities before we have spent much time with them. I want to compare those family relationships while remembering that a kid’s interpretation of a parent may be mistaken.
  • The irony has levels, apparently. The description of Bro’s Mr. T puppet piles one level of irony onto another. Later, Dave finds some of Bro’s material too ironic to deal with. I am interested in where ironic distance becomes indistinguishable from sincere investment.
  • Alchemy as a storytelling principle. The comic keeps making new things by combining familiar ones, from prototyped sprites to alchemized household objects. Even an object’s name can change what the inventory does: Dave calls his skateboard a “wheeled ride” to avoid an inventory collision. A change in wording becomes a practical solution.

Discussion questions

  1. Which character has made the strongest impression on you so far, and why?
  2. Which running joke still makes you laugh, and which has worn thin?
  3. What do the kids’ rooms tell us about them that their conversations do not?
  4. Do you trust the kids’ accounts of their guardians? What makes you trust or doubt them?
  5. When does irony help the friends connect, and when does it keep them at a distance?
  6. Who seems to be giving the commands: the characters, the narrator, the reader, or someone inside the story? What makes you think so?
  7. What changes when an apocalypse is presented through the rules and jokes of a game?

Trivia & Details

  • The fake literary credits are a running joke. The fireplace passage credits Mark Twain, but the moon stealing its light comes from Shakespeare’s Timon of Athens, Act 4, scene 3. Rose’s rainy introduction credits Charles Barkley for the opening of T. S. Eliot’s The Waste Land. The narrator’s certainty is part of the joke.
  • [S] starts small. The piano page explicitly introduces [S] as the marker for sound. Compare that early cue with the act-ending animation, [S] WV: Ascend.
  • A word can change the mechanics. Dave gets his battery pack accepted by treating Y as a consonant instead of a vowel. The punchline depends on wording, rather than a new item or a stronger weapon.
